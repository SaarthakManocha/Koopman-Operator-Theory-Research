\begin{abstract}
Shortest Job First (SJF) scheduling needs the length of each task's next Central Processing Unit (CPU) burst before the burst runs. This paper predicts bursts with a Koopman operator, fitted by Dynamic Mode Decomposition (DMD) on log-scale delay coordinates, and feeds the predictions to the unmodified Linux Earliest Eligible Virtual Deadline First (EEVDF) scheduler as per-task slice requests. Every measurement is validated against the kernel's own accounting, on a virtual machine (CPU time within 0.93\%, 105 processes) and on a 28-CPU bare-metal machine (within 0.44\% for every process with at least 0.5\,s of CPU time, 903 processes, no lost events). On real programs (Redis, SQLite, Node.js, ffmpeg) the predictor beats the textbook exponential average in 32 of 36 threads, with 29\% lower error. The predictor is shown to be numerically a log-scale autoregressive model, and neither nonlinear observables, an Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA) model nor a neural network beats that model. In closed loop on bare metal with 1, 8 and 16 CPUs, an Extended Berkeley Packet Filter (eBPF) observer that costs at most 1.4\% of one core feeds the predictor, and predicted slices cut the response time of short requests by 57--59\% with no loss of throughput; the Koopman predictor beats the exponential average in 48 of 54 paired runs. Slices need a margin above the predicted burst: a slice exactly equal to the true burst gives only 8--9\%, while 1.5 times the burst gives 72\%. With real servers sharing a core with CPU-bound tasks on a second bare-metal machine, Redis requests became 43\% faster, SQLite point queries 77\% and Node.js light requests 34\%, although Redis's 99th-percentile latency rose by 21\%. A complete sched\_ext scheduler built on the same predictor cut the response time of short requests by 44\% and of long requests by 41\% against stock Linux. The capture kit, data, controller, sched\_ext scheduler and a scheduler visualizer are released.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
%======================================================================
Shortest Job First (SJF) scheduling minimizes the mean waiting time on one processor, and its preemptive form, Shortest Remaining Time First (SRTF), does the same when jobs arrive over time~\cite{silberschatz2018}. Both need something no operating system has: the length of each task's next CPU burst before it runs. Standard operating-systems textbooks therefore teach an estimate of it, the \emph{exponential average} of the previous bursts~\cite{silberschatz2018,tanenbaum2015}, which Section~\ref{sec:textbook} explains; this paper calls it the \emph{textbook estimator}. Production schedulers have avoided prediction altogether. The Linux kernel's default scheduler from kernel version 2.6.23 (2007) until kernel version 6.6 was the Completely Fair Scheduler (CFS), which always runs the task that has received the least CPU time so far~\cite{cfsdoc}. In kernel version 6.6 (October 2023) its task-selection logic was replaced by Earliest Eligible Virtual Deadline First (EEVDF)~\cite{stoica1995,corbet2023eevdf}, which serves tasks in order of virtual deadlines that depend on how much CPU time they have received and on a time slice, not on how long their next burst will be.

Since kernel version 6.12 (November 2024) the slice is no longer fixed. A task may request its own slice through the \texttt{sched\_setattr} system call, and a shorter slice gives it an earlier virtual deadline and the right to preempt tasks with longer slices~\cite{corbet2024,eevdfdoc}. This turns burst prediction from a textbook exercise into a lever on a production scheduler. If a task's next burst is known to be short, its slice can be set short, and EEVDF will serve it sooner; that is SJF expressed inside the stock kernel, with no patch and no new scheduling class. Whether this works depends on two questions that, to the author's knowledge, have not been answered with real kernel data: how well can bursts be predicted, and does a better prediction make the real scheduler faster?

This paper studies both with Koopman operator methods~\cite{koopman1931,mezic2005,brunton2022}. A Koopman operator represents a nonlinear system by a linear operator acting on functions of its state; Dynamic Mode Decomposition (DMD) and Extended Dynamic Mode Decomposition (EDMD) estimate it from data~\cite{schmid2010,williams2015}, and its eigenvalues describe how patterns in the data decay or oscillate. Burst sequences are short, noisy and nonstationary, which suits a model that is linear in a well-chosen space, can be updated online in nanoseconds, and comes with a spectrum that describes each task. Koopman models on delay coordinates are, however, closely related to autoregressive models~\cite{arbabi2017,pan2020}, so a third question is what the Koopman view adds beyond them.

\textbf{Approach.} A measurement pipeline was built first, and every reconstructed quantity was validated against counters that the kernel keeps independently; this exposed two hazards that would otherwise have corrupted the data. Burst predictors were then compared on synthetic workloads, a bare-metal trace and four real programs, against ten baselines. Finally the loop was closed: a controller observes each task's bursts, predicts the next one and sets its EEVDF slice, first by polling the \texttt{/proc} file system and then through an Extended Berkeley Packet Filter (eBPF) program attached to the scheduler's tracepoint. The controller was evaluated in 504 paired runs: 264 on a two-core virtual machine, 180 on a 28-CPU bare-metal machine with 1, 8 and 16 CPUs in use, and 60 with real servers on a second, 8-CPU bare-metal machine. A sched\_ext scheduler driven by the same predictor was then evaluated in 36 further runs.

\textbf{Findings.} (i) On real programs, a log-scale Koopman predictor beats the textbook estimator in 32 of 36 threads, with 29\% lower error. (ii) It is numerically a log-scale autoregressive model, and neither nonlinear Koopman dictionaries, an Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA) model nor a neural network beats an autoregressive model with enough delays: what predicts CPU bursts is the log scale and the delay depth. (iii) Fed to EEVDF as slice requests, predictions cut the response time of short requests by 57--59\% on bare metal with 1, 8 and 16 CPUs at negligible cost, and the latency of real servers' short requests by 34--77\%. (iv) How a prediction becomes a slice matters more than which predictor is used: a slice exactly equal to the true burst gives only 8--9\%, because the burst overruns its slice, while 1.5 times the burst gives 72\%. A short model explains this, and also predicts where a more accurate predictor helps, which the experiments confirm. (v) The same predictor can drive a complete scheduler: a sched\_ext policy that orders tasks by virtual time plus predicted burst beat stock EEVDF by 44\% on short requests and 41\% on long ones, while the same policy without predictions was far worse than stock Linux.

\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item A validated measurement pipeline for scheduler dynamics: trace capture without perturbation, ground truth from the kernel's \texttt{schedstat} counters, self-tests, and two documented measurement hazards (Section~\ref{sec:measure}).
\item A burst-prediction study on synthetic workloads, a bare-metal trace and four real programs, against autoregressive, ARIMA, neural, gradient-boosting and nonlinear-Koopman baselines, with prediction intervals and per-prediction cost (Section~\ref{sec:pred}).
\item A closed-loop controller that steers the unmodified EEVDF scheduler through slice requests, with an eBPF burst observer, a model of the slice margin, and an evaluation in 504 paired runs on synthetic and real workloads, on a virtual machine and two bare-metal machines (Sections~\ref{sec:method} and~\ref{sec:closed}).
\item A complete sched\_ext scheduler driven by the predictor, evaluated on bare metal (Section~\ref{sec:scx}).
\item A multi-core analysis, a machine-level Koopman change detector and an interactive trace visualizer (Sections~\ref{sec:multicore}--\ref{sec:viz}).
\end{enumerate}
Section~\ref{sec:apps} summarizes applications of the same estimator above the operating system, and all code and data are released.

\section{Background}
%======================================================================
\subsection{The textbook burst estimator}\label{sec:textbook}
Operating-systems textbooks introduce burst prediction together with SJF scheduling~\cite{silberschatz2018,tanenbaum2015}. The length of the next CPU burst is not known in advance, but bursts of the same process tend to be similar in length, so the next one is predicted from the lengths of the previous ones by the \emph{exponential average}
\begin{equation}
\tau_{n+1}=\alpha\,t_n+(1-\alpha)\,\tau_n ,\qquad 0\le\alpha\le1,
\end{equation}
where $t_n$ is the length of the $n$-th (most recent) burst, $\tau_n$ the previous prediction and $\tau_{n+1}$ the prediction for the next burst; the first prediction $\tau_0$ is a constant, such as a system-wide average. The parameter $\alpha$ sets the weight of recent against past history: with $\alpha=0$ the latest burst is ignored, with $\alpha=1$ only the latest burst counts, and the common textbook choice $\alpha=1/2$ weights the latest burst and the past history equally. Expanding the recursion,
\begin{equation}
\tau_{n+1}=\alpha t_n+(1-\alpha)\alpha t_{n-1}+\dots+(1-\alpha)^{j}\alpha\, t_{n-j}+\dots+(1-\alpha)^{n+1}\tau_0 ,
\end{equation}
so each older burst counts geometrically less than the one after it~\cite{silberschatz2018}. Tanenbaum and Bos describe the same technique as \emph{aging}, with $\alpha=1/2$, which can be computed with one addition and one shift~\cite{tanenbaum2015}. In statistics and forecasting the same recursion is known as exponential smoothing~\cite{brown1959} or the Exponentially Weighted Moving Average (EWMA)~\cite{roberts1959}.

Throughout this paper, the \emph{textbook estimator} or \emph{textbook EWMA} means this exponential average with $\alpha=0.5$, the form taught in these textbooks; a variant with $\alpha$ tuned on training data is reported as well. It is the natural baseline: it is what an operating-systems course proposes for SJF, and it is cheap enough to run inside a scheduler. Its weakness, examined in Section~\ref{sec:pred}, is that it averages on a linear scale, so a single long burst pulls the next few predictions far upwards.

\subsection{EEVDF and slice requests}
EEVDF~\cite{stoica1995} gives each runnable task $i$ a virtual runtime $v_i$, which advances by the CPU time it receives divided by its weight. The run queue's average $V=\sum_i w_iv_i/\sum_i w_i$ defines each task's \emph{lag} $V-v_i$, the service it is owed. A task is \emph{eligible} when its lag is non-negative, and its virtual deadline is
\begin{equation}
d_i = v_i + \frac{s_i}{w_i},
\end{equation}
where $s_i$ is its slice. The scheduler runs the eligible task with the earliest deadline. A task that sleeps keeps its lag, so on waking it is placed at $v_i=V-\text{lag}_i$, and a task that has slept while others ran is usually eligible at once. When a running task has used its slice, its deadline is renewed, $d_i\leftarrow v_i+s_i/w_i$, and the scheduler picks again.

Two features matter here. With \emph{run to parity}, the task picked keeps the CPU until its slice is used, even if another task becomes more deserving, which limits context switches. With slice requests (kernel version 6.12)~\cite{corbet2024,eevdfdoc}, any task may set $s_i$ between 0.1\,ms and 100\,ms through the \texttt{sched\_runtime} field of \texttt{sched\_setattr}; a woken task whose slice is shorter than the running task's, and whose deadline is earlier, may preempt it at once. The default slice on the test machine is 1.4\,ms. A shorter slice is therefore a request to be served sooner, paid for by more frequent preemption if the burst does not fit.

\subsection{Koopman operators and Dynamic Mode Decomposition}
For a discrete-time system $x_{t+1}=F(x_t)$, the Koopman operator $\mathcal{K}$ acts on observables $g$ of the state by composition, $(\mathcal{K}g)(x)=g(F(x))$~\cite{koopman1931}. It is linear even when $F$ is not, at the price of acting on a space of functions. Its eigenfunctions evolve by multiplication with their eigenvalue, so the eigenvalues describe growth, decay and oscillation of patterns in the data~\cite{mezic2005}. EDMD~\cite{williams2015} approximates $\mathcal{K}$ on a finite dictionary $\psi$ of observables by the matrix
\begin{equation}
\K=\arg\min_A\|\Psi_Y-\Psi_XA\|_F=\Psi_X^{+}\Psi_Y ,
\end{equation}
where the rows of $\Psi_X$ and $\Psi_Y$ are $\psi(x_t)$ and $\psi(x_{t+1})$. With $\psi(x)=x$, EDMD is DMD~\cite{schmid2010,tu2014}; as the data grow it converges to the projection of $\mathcal{K}$ onto the span of the dictionary~\cite{korda2018}. When only a scalar is measured, delay coordinates $(y_t,y_{t-1},\dots)$ reconstruct the state (Takens' theorem)~\cite{takens1981}, and DMD on them (Hankel DMD) approximates the Koopman spectrum for ergodic systems~\cite{arbabi2017}. Linear models on delay coordinates include autoregressive models and Hankel DMD as special cases~\cite{pan2020,brunton2017havok}. Measurement noise biases DMD eigenvalues towards zero~\cite{dawson2016}. Koopman models have been used for prediction and control in fluids, power systems and finance~\cite{kutz2016,mann2016,kordampc2018}, and learned dictionaries extend them with neural networks~\cite{lusch2018}.

\subsection{Extended Berkeley Packet Filter and sched\_ext}
Extended Berkeley Packet Filter (eBPF) programs run verified code inside the kernel at tracepoints and other hooks, and communicate with user space through maps and ring buffers~\cite{gregg2019}. sched\_ext (kernel version 6.12) lets an eBPF program implement a complete scheduling policy, with the kernel reverting to its built-in scheduler if the program fails~\cite{schedext}.

\section{Related Work}
%======================================================================
\textbf{Burst prediction for scheduling.} The exponential average of Section~\ref{sec:textbook} is the standard textbook estimator~\cite{silberschatz2018,tanenbaum2015}. Negi and Kumar predicted CPU bursts of known programs from static and dynamic process attributes with decision trees and set Linux~2.4 time slices from the predictions, reducing turnaround time by 1.4--5.8\%~\cite{negi2005}. That work required a modified kernel and per-program training; the present work uses the stock scheduler's slice interface and predicts each task from its own recent bursts. Size-based scheduling has long been known to help workloads with heavy-tailed job sizes~\cite{harchol2003}.

\textbf{Learned and pluggable schedulers.} Reinforcement learning has been used to schedule jobs in data-processing clusters~\cite{mao2019decima}. On a single machine, sched\_ext has made experimental policies easy to deploy~\cite{schedext}; a recent system uses a learned workload classifier to switch between whole sched\_ext policies at run time~\cite{wang2025mos}. The present work instead keeps the stock scheduler and changes only the slice it is asked to use. The Linux scheduler has a history of subtle performance bugs that only careful measurement revealed~\cite{lozi2016}, which motivates the emphasis on validation here.

\textbf{Koopman methods.} Koopman operators have been applied to prediction and control of physical and financial systems~\cite{kutz2016,mann2016,kordampc2018,brunton2022}. The relation between DMD on delay coordinates and autoregressive models is known~\cite{arbabi2017,pan2020}, and classical time-series practice selects the autoregressive order by information criteria~\cite{box2015,akaike1974}. The author is not aware of Koopman methods applied to operating-system scheduling; this paper tests the method against these classical alternatives rather than assuming an advantage.

\section{Method}\label{sec:method}
%======================================================================
\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{a1_pipeline.png}
\caption{Overview. Top: offline study of traced bursts and predictors. Bottom: the closed loop on the live kernel, in which an eBPF program reports each burst, a per-task operator predicts the next one, and the prediction becomes an EEVDF slice request.}\label{fig:pipeline}

Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline} shows the two parts of the study. This section defines the predictor, the mapping from prediction to slice, and the model that explains why that mapping needs a margin.

\subsection{Burst predictor}
A \emph{burst} is the CPU time a task uses between two moments at which it blocks or sleeps; preemptions do not end a burst. For each task, the state is its last $p$ bursts on a log scale,
\begin{equation}
z_t=\big(\log b_t,\ \log b_{t-1},\ \dots,\ \log b_{t-p+1}\big),
\end{equation}
standardized with training statistics, and a single operator is fitted by least squares,
\begin{equation}
\K=\arg\min_A\sum_t\|z_{t+1}-z_tA\|^2 .
\end{equation}
The predicted next burst is the first coordinate of $z_t\K$, mapped back from the log scale. The delay depth is $p=3$ unless stated otherwise. The log scale matters: burst lengths span several orders of magnitude, and on a linear scale a few long bursts dominate the least-squares fit and every forecast.

Because the dictionary is linear in delay coordinates, the forecast equals that of a log-scale Autoregressive (AR) model of order $p$, AR($p$), fitted by least squares; the two differ only through centring and an intercept. Across the 96 tasks studied, the median of the largest difference between their log-scale forecasts is $10^{-3}$. The predictor is called log-EDMD below. Two nonlinear dictionaries that an AR model cannot express are also tested: the lags plus their squares and pairwise products (\emph{poly-EDMD}), and the lags plus 64 Random Fourier Features (RFF) $\sqrt{2/D}\cos(z_tW+b)$ with Gaussian $W$, an approximation of kernel EDMD (\emph{RFF-EDMD}).

\textbf{Online form.} In the controller each task's operator is updated after every burst by recursive least squares with an intercept and forgetting factor $\lambda=0.995$. With regressor $x_t=(1,\log b_t,\log b_{t-1},\log b_{t-2})$ and target $y=\log b_{t+1}$,
\begin{align}
g&=\frac{P x_t}{\lambda+x_t^\top Px_t}, \qquad \theta \leftarrow \theta+g\,(y-\theta^\top x_t),\\
P&\leftarrow \big(P-g\,x_t^\top P\big)/\lambda ,
\end{align}
and the next prediction is $\exp(\theta^\top x_{t+1})$. Until a task has ten bursts, the textbook estimator is used.

\textbf{Spectrum.} The eigenvalues of $\K$ summarize a task's burst dynamics: a dominant eigenvalue near one indicates a slowly decaying level, complex pairs indicate alternation, and eigenvalues near zero indicate bursts close to independent.

\subsection{From prediction to slice}
The slice requested for a task is
\begin{equation}
s=\min\big(\max(c\,\hat b,\ s_{\min}),\ s_{\text{def}}\big),
\end{equation}
where $\hat b$ is the predicted burst, $c$ a margin, $s_{\min}=0.1$\,ms the kernel minimum and $s_{\text{def}}$ the default slice (1.4\,ms here). The controller only ever shortens slices. Algorithm~\ref{alg:ctl} gives the loop.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Slice controller (eBPF version)}\label{alg:ctl}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{eBPF program, on} \texttt{sched\_switch}(prev, next, prev\_state):
\State \quad if prev is tracked: acc[prev] += now $-$ on[prev]
\State \quad \quad if prev\_state $\neq$ RUNNING: emit (prev, acc[prev]); acc[prev] $\leftarrow 0$
\State \quad if next is tracked: on[next] $\leftarrow$ now
\State \textbf{User space, for each event} (task $i$, burst $b$):
\State \quad update task $i$'s operator with $b$ (recursive least squares)
\State \quad $\hat b \leftarrow$ predicted next burst of task $i$
\State \quad $s \leftarrow \min(\max(c\hat b,\ s_{\min}),\ s_{\text{def}})$
\State \quad \texttt{sched\_setattr}($i$): \texttt{sched\_runtime} $\leftarrow s$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Why the slice needs a margin}\label{sec:margin}
Consider a request task that wakes while CPU-bound tasks with the default slice share its core. Its response time $R$, from wake-up to the end of its burst, has three parts. The \emph{wait} $W(s)$ before it first runs grows with its slice $s$: its deadline is $v+s$, so a smaller $s$ makes it more likely to preempt the running task at once and to be picked first. Its \emph{CPU demand} $b'$ is the burst $b$ plus system overhead (wake-up path, system calls, accounting). And if $b'>s$, its deadline is renewed while it still runs and it loses its place to tasks whose deadlines are now earlier, adding a \emph{penalty} $\Delta$ of the order of their slices. Thus
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}[R\mid s]\ \approx\ W(s)+b'+\Delta\,\Pr(b'>s).
\end{equation}
With $s=c\hat b$ and a log-normal prediction error $\log(b/\hat b)\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)$, and with small overhead,
\begin{equation}
\Pr(b'>s)\ \approx\ 1-\Phi\!\left(\frac{\log c}{\sigma}\right).
\end{equation}
The first term pushes $c$ down and the third pushes it up, so there is an interior optimum. Two predictions follow. First, even a perfect predictor ($\sigma=0$) needs $c>1$, because $b'>b$: a slice of exactly the burst should overrun often and give little or no gain. Second, at a fixed margin, a predictor with smaller $\sigma$ overruns less; so differences between predictors should appear at small $c$, where overruns are frequent, and vanish at large $c$, where every slice saturates at the default. Section~\ref{sec:closed} tests both.

\subsection{Uncertainty}
A scheduler can act on an interval instead of a point. Split conformal prediction~\cite{vovk2005,angelopoulos2023} is used for this: on a calibration set, the scores $|\log(b/\hat b)|$ give a quantile $q$, and the interval $[\hat b e^{-q},\ \hat b e^{q}]$ covers a new burst with probability at least $1-a$ if bursts are exchangeable. Bursts are a time series, so the coverage is measured rather than assumed.

All run EEVDF.

\textbf{Workloads.} Synthetic workloads are small programs: a CPU-bound loop; an Input/Output (I/O)-bound loop that writes 10\,kB and sleeps 50\,ms; and a \emph{phased} positive control that alternates every 2\,s between bursts of 1\,ms and 20\,ms of CPU time, so its bursts have known structure. The lab trace (\emph{Trace~3}) ran three CPU-bound and three I/O-bound jobs. VM captures ran these plus two phased jobs, pinned to 1 or 2 cores, overloaded, or with a scheduled load drop, each three times. Real programs and the closed-loop workloads are described in Sections~\ref{sec:pred} and~\ref{sec:closed}.

\textbf{Reconstruction.} From the \texttt{sched\_switch}, \texttt{sched\_wakeup} and \texttt{sched\_migrate\_task} tracepoints, every task's history is rebuilt: every interval on a CPU with its state at switch-out (preempted, sleeping or waiting on disk), every interval waiting in a run queue after a wake-up or a preemption, and every migration. Consecutive intervals that end in preemption are merged into one burst.

\textbf{Ground truth.} Each workload saves, just before it exits, the kernel's own \texttt{/proc/self/schedstat} (CPU time, run-queue wait and number of times scheduled) and its voluntary and involuntary context-switch counts. These are maintained by the scheduler independently of tracing. Over 105 processes in 12 VM captures (Fig.~\ref{fig:valid}), reconstructed CPU time differs from the kernel's by 0.93\% (median; maximum 3.45\%), wait time by 0.22\% (maximum 2.07\%), and the number of times scheduled by a median of 4 out of thousands, with no lost events. The small excess of traced CPU time comes from the exit path after the counters are read and from interrupt time, which the kernel's per-task accounting excludes.

The 42 bare-metal captures of the second lab machine (903 processes; Section~\ref{sec:multicore}) were validated in the same way. CPU time agrees with the kernel within 0.44\% for every process that used at least 0.5\,s of CPU (median 0.02\%), the number of times scheduled agrees exactly at the median, and no events were lost. The trace exceeds the kernel by a nearly constant 4\,ms per process (median), the exit path; for I/O jobs that use only about 40\,ms of CPU in a run this is a large fraction, so their agreement is better judged in absolute terms (all within 20\,ms). On cores that are often idle the trace counts up to about 12\,$\mu$s more wait per wake-up than the kernel's run-queue delay; with every core busy the two agree within 1\,$\mu$s per wake-up. The bare-metal scaling results below are therefore taken from the kernel's own counters.

\textbf{Hazard 1: the observer effect.} A first capture tool streamed events out of the kernel while recording. Each event woke the reader, which preempted the workload and produced further events: a feedback loop of about 50{,}000 context switches per second on any core with spare capacity. Traces grew from 0.5--0.8\,MB to 15--23\,MB and counts disagreed with the kernel by up to 9\%. Recording into a large kernel buffer and reading it once after the run removed the loop. One contaminated capture is kept as evidence.

\textbf{Hazard 2: missing idle exits on a VM.} On the Intel VM's kernel, part of the \texttt{sched\_switch} events for a CPU leaving idle are never emitted; tracefs and perf agree, and the ring buffer reports no loss. In seven self-tests a task that slept 201 times was seen switching back in only 1 to 176 times. None of the lab machines shows it: there were zero unmatched switch-outs in Trace~3, in all 42 captures of the second lab machine and in the 3 captures of the third, and their self-tests saw every switch back in (204 of 204 and 201 of 201). The cause was not determined. The parser therefore counts unmatched switch-outs, the capture tool runs a self-test, and VM experiments keep every core busy, either with workload or with one lowest-priority (\texttt{SCHED\_IDLE}) spinner per core, which receives only CPU time nothing else wants. With busy cores the counts match the kernel: in a 15\,s real-program test without spinners there were 1{,}639 unmatched switch-outs, and with them 186 across three 60\,s captures and 310{,}000 timeslices.

\section{Burst Prediction}\label{sec:pred}
%======================================================================
\subsection{Protocol}
Every method predicts a burst from earlier bursts of the same task only; the last 20\% of each task is the test set and everything is fitted on the rest. Methods: training mean and median; last value; the textbook estimator (EWMA with $\alpha=0.5$, Section~\ref{sec:textbook}) and EWMA with $\alpha$ tuned on training data; log-EDMD, poly-EDMD and RFF-EDMD; recursive (multi-step) EDMD; log-AR with order 1--10 chosen by the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC)~\cite{akaike1974} (log-AR($p^*$)); ARIMA($p,d,q$) with $p\le2$, $d\le1$, $q\le1$ chosen by AIC~\cite{box2015}; a neural network with two hidden layers of 32 units on six log lags; and gradient boosting on three raw lags. Errors are mean absolute errors; ratios to a reference are computed per task. Intervals are 95\% moving-block bootstrap intervals with blocks of 10~\cite{kunsch1989}; across tasks, sign tests with Holm correction are used~\cite{holm1979}.

\subsection{Synthetic workloads and the bare-metal trace}
\textbf{Uncontended bursts (Trace~3).} On the idle lab machine (on average 2.3 of 28 cores busy), CPU-bound bursts ended only because kernel threads preempted them: all 769 preemptions came from \texttt{kworker} (55\%) or \texttt{migration} threads (45\%). I/O bursts roughly tripled, from 85--113\,$\mu$s to 320--340\,$\mu$s, on the \emph{same} cores when the CPU-bound jobs exited (Fig.~\ref{fig:trace3}). Power management was suspected, and a later test on the second lab machine supports it. The same workload (three CPU-bound jobs for 40\,s, I/O and phased jobs for 70\,s, all cores) ran three times with the default CPU frequency governor and three times with the governor fixed at \texttt{performance}. With the default governor, the I/O bursts lengthened in 9 of 9 processes once the CPU-bound jobs exited, by a median factor of 1.65 (median burst 61\,$\mu$s before, 101\,$\mu$s after). With the \texttt{performance} governor the lengthening disappeared (59\,$\mu$s before, 57\,$\mu$s after; median per-process ratio 0.87). The effect is smaller than the threefold change in Trace~3, which was recorded on a different machine, but it has the same direction and depends on the power-management setting. This level shift dominates I/O forecasting: methods that follow recent bursts cut error by 52--83\% against a constant, and the textbook estimator was best or tied. The I/O operators' dominant eigenvalues, 0.88--0.97, are the Koopman signature of the shift, and recursive EDMD forecasts decay to the mean, as eigenvalues inside the unit circle imply.

\subsection{Real programs}
To test beyond synthetic loops, the following programs were traced together on both VM cores in three captures of 60\,s: a Redis~7.0 server driven by a paced client (mostly GET and SET, with occasional range and sort commands on a 30{,}000-element list); a Node.js~22 Hypertext Transfer Protocol (HTTP) server whose client alternates between phases of light and heavy JavaScript Object Notation (JSON) requests; a Python process running Structured Query Language (SQL) queries of mixed size (point lookups, range aggregates, full scans) against SQLite~3.45; real-time H.264 encoding of a synthetic video with ffmpeg (all encoder threads); and the two load generators. A parallel gcc build and xz compression ran as untracked background load. Over the 36 threads with at least 60 bursts, CPU time agrees with \texttt{schedstat} within 1.8\% and wait time within 1.5\% (medians); 28 threads are within 5\%, and the others are ffmpeg helper threads that run about 12\,$\mu$s per slice, where a few microseconds of tracepoint overhead is a large fraction.

Log-EDMD beats the textbook estimator in 32 of 36 threads, with median error ratio 0.71 ($p_\text{Holm}=10^{-5}$; Table~\ref{tab:pred}, Fig.~\ref{fig:real}). The gain is largest for Redis and SQLite (median ratios 0.55 and 0.56), whose request bursts are short with occasional long ones: the exponential average chases each long burst and then overestimates the short ones that follow, while the log-scale operator treats a long burst as a large but transient deviation. The same four programs were captured three times on the 8-CPU bare-metal machine. For the 24 threads that used at least 0.5\,s of CPU, traced CPU time agreed with the kernel within 3.1\% (median 0.33\%), with no lost events; idle ffmpeg helper threads, with a few tens of microseconds of CPU each, are not comparable. Log-EDMD beat the textbook estimator in 24 of 30 threads (median error ratio 0.81, sign test $p=0.0014$), again most for Redis and SQLite (0.55 and 0.56, as on the VM) and the load generators (0.71), and least for ffmpeg (0.94). The spectral picture repeated too: SQLite's eigenvalues were again real, and eigenvalues identified the program a window came from with 77\% balanced accuracy, against 99\% for simple burst statistics.

%======================================================================
\section{Closing the Loop: Steering EEVDF}\label{sec:closed}
%======================================================================
The controller was first developed and evaluated on the two-core Intel VM (Sections~\ref{sec:poll} and~\ref{sec:vmebpf}); the eBPF experiment was then repeated on the bare-metal lab machine with 1, 8 and 16 CPUs (Section~\ref{sec:bare}). Real servers were steered on the VM (Section~\ref{sec:realloop}) and on a second bare-metal machine (Section~\ref{sec:realbare}), and Section~\ref{sec:scx} replaces the scheduler itself with a sched\_ext policy driven by the same predictor.

\subsection{Set-up}
\textbf{Workload.} Request tasks, written in C, wake on a fixed period of 15, 20 or 25\,ms and do one CPU burst each, timed with the thread CPU clock. Burst lengths come from a two-regime log-autoregressive process (coefficient 0.6, noise 0.35): short bursts near 0.3\,ms and long ones near 4\,ms, switching regime with probability 0.05 per request. Each request logs its deadline, wake-up time and finish time; if it finishes after the next deadline, the next request starts at once. CPU hogs (CPU-bound background tasks) spin and count the CPU time they receive, which measures throughput. All tasks are pinned to the CPUs under test.

\textbf{Design.} Every repetition runs every policy with the same random seeds, in shuffled order, so comparisons are paired, and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests~\cite{wilcoxon1945} are used. The tasks' own CPU accounting agrees with \texttt{schedstat} within about 1\% (median per policy). The main metric is the mean response time of short requests (under 1\,ms of CPU), from deadline to finish; long requests, all requests, wake-up latency, context switches and hog throughput are also reported.

\textbf{Policies.} Stock Linux (default slice for every task); a 0.1\,ms slice for every task (\emph{fixed}); a 0.1\,ms slice for request tasks only, set by hand (\emph{hand-labelled}); predictors setting slices from the textbook estimator (EWMA) or log-EDMD, with margin $c$ or with a conformal margin (the prediction times the 90th percentile of the task's recent error ratios); and oracles, in which each task sets its own slice to $c$ times its true next burst.

Log-EDMD was 25\% better than the textbook estimator on SQLite point queries (10 of 10 pairs) and 10\% worse on Redis (1 of 10). Heavy requests, the overall mean of Node.js and SQLite, and the 99th percentiles of all three programs changed by less than 4\% under either predictor (not significant), and hogs lost 1\% of their CPU time. A hand-set minimum slice for the three servers did best for SQLite point queries ($-54\%$) and Node.js light requests ($-28\%$) but worst for Redis ($-16\%$, with its 99th percentile 9\% higher), and it requires knowing in advance which tasks are latency-sensitive; the predictors needed no such knowledge.

Slice requests change only one input of EEVDF. The release also contains \texttt{scx\_koopman}, a complete sched\_ext scheduler in which the prediction decides the order as well: an eBPF program keeps one global queue ordered by virtual time plus predicted next burst, measures every burst, and gives each task a slice of the margin times its prediction, while a user-space agent updates each task's log-scale operator by recursive least squares, as in Algorithm~\ref{alg:ctl}. Tasks without a prediction get the sched\_ext default slice of 20\,ms. It was run on the 8-CPU bare-metal machine with 24 hogs and 32 request tasks, six paired repetitions of six policies (Table~\ref{tab:scx}). The kernel loaded and unloaded it cleanly in all 30 runs that used it.

With predictions it beat stock EEVDF on every request type. The price is context switches, 5.6 times as many as stock Linux. The margin behaves as in Section~\ref{sec:margin}: a margin of 1 was best for short requests but cost long ones, and margins of 1.5--2 balanced the two.

\section{Multi-Core Scheduling}\label{sec:multicore}
%======================================================================
\textbf{Measured latency.} On the lab machine, processes waited a median 16\,$\mu$s for a CPU; no queue formed, so the order in which a scheduler picks tasks could not matter. On one VM core the same kind of workload waited a median 0.07\,ms (I/O) and 2.6\,ms (phased) after waking, with 95th percentiles of 2.6 and 7.9\,ms. On the ARM VM, using only the kernel's per-process counters, CPU-bound jobs spent 76\% of their time waiting on one core, 50\% on two and 1\% on four (Fig.~\ref{fig:scale}); the Intel VM traces give the same values on one and two cores.

\textbf{Bare-metal scaling.} On the second lab machine the same eight jobs (three CPU-bound, three I/O-bound, two phased) ran on 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 and all 28 CPUs, three times each, and the kernel's own counters give the waiting (Fig.~\ref{fig:labscale}, left). CPU-bound jobs spent 76.5\% of their time waiting on one CPU, 50.2\% on two, 0.05\% on four and none from eight up, the same values as on both virtual machines. A second series added one CPU-bound job per CPU, so that every core always had CPU-bound work (Fig.~\ref{fig:labscale}, right). The phased interactive jobs then waited 1.75--1.77\,ms per run at 4, 8 and 16 CPUs and 1.56\,ms at 28, against under 1\,$\mu$s per run when 8 or more cores were free. Adding cores does not reduce the waiting of interactive tasks when every core already holds CPU-bound work: what matters is the order in which each core picks its tasks, and that is what a slice request changes. Under overload (two CPU-bound jobs per CPU) the interactive jobs waited 2.6\,ms (phased) and 0.18\,ms (I/O) per run.

\textbf{Trace-driven simulation.} Each traced task is replayed as a closed loop of its real CPU bursts and blocking times on $m$ identical cores with one shared queue, under First-Come, First-Served (FCFS), Round Robin (3\,ms time quantum), non-preemptive SJF and preemptive SRTF, each with a predictor (running mean, textbook EWMA, online log-EDMD or exact lengths). A job that has run past its prediction is assumed to need at least as long again. Replaying Trace~3 (Fig.~\ref{fig:cores}), interactive response time is zero for every policy from four cores up, one more than the number of CPU-bound jobs. Below that, FCFS and non-preemptive SJF make interactive jobs wait 24--77\,ms on average whatever the predictor, because a job that arrives while a CPU-bound job holds the core must wait for it. SRTF with a predictor does not help on this trace at one or two cores: each CPU-bound job runs a single long burst, so no predictor has history to tell it apart; only exact lengths do (0.01\,ms). In the VM's two-core captures, where every job has history, SRTF with any predictor cuts mean interactive response from 10.4\,ms (FCFS) and 10.2\,ms (SJF) to 0.011\,ms. On one core, measured EEVDF gave interactive jobs 1.87\,ms, against 1.5--1.9\,ms for simulated SRTF with predictors, 6.8\,ms for Round Robin and 16.4\,ms for FCFS. The simulator omits switch and migration costs and per-core queues. Prediction pays only where tasks queue, and then through preemption, which is what slice requests provide.

\section{Koopman View of the Whole Machine}\label{sec:detect}
%======================================================================
The same estimator, applied to a state describing the whole machine, gives a change detector: the one-step residual $r_t=(\|z_{t+1}-z_t\K\|-\bar r)/s_r$, in units of the training residual's mean and spread, averaged causally over a short window, raises an alarm when it stays above a threshold for a hold time.

\textbf{Offline, from traces.} The state every 10\,ms is the busy fraction and queued tasks of each core plus the CPU share of each job class. In three VM runs where four of six CPU-bound jobs stop at 20\,s while every core stays busy, an operator fitted on the first 8\,s raised an alarm 77--94\,ms after the change in all three, with no false alarm before it or in nine runs without a change (Fig.~\ref{fig:regime}). A persistence residual and a distance-from-normal score on the same inputs detected none of the three; a utilization monitor cannot, because utilization stays at 100\%. On the 28-core lab trace, a four-variable machine-wide state detected the first CPU-bound exit 32\,ms after it without a false alarm (distance-from-normal: 52\,ms).

\section{Beyond the Operating System}\label{sec:apps}
%======================================================================
The same estimator, a least-squares operator on a standardized state, has also been applied above the operating system. Two applications are summarized here as case studies of the method.

\textbf{Network traffic.} In earlier work~\cite{drishti2026}, 16.2 million CSE-CIC-IDS2018 flow records~\cite{sharafaldin2018} were binned into 9{,}777 windows of 30\,s, each summarized by an 11-dimensional state: ports targeted, protocol diversity, flow count, forward and backward volume and their asymmetry, mean and spread of inter-arrival time, and SYN, RST and FIN counts. One $11\times11$ operator was fitted on 6{,}871 training transitions that do not cross capture gaps. On the chronologically held-out final day (1{,}046 transitions) it lowered mean squared forecast error against persistence by 25.9\% at 30\,s, 29.1\% at 60\,s and 18.9\% at 15\,min, and the same construction lowered it by 20.7\% on UNSW-NB15~\cite{moustafa2015}. All eleven eigenvalues are real and inside the unit circle; the largest, 0.995, gives a slowest-mode half-life of 71\,min.

\textbf{Equity markets.} EIGENWORLD~\cite{eigenworld} builds diffusion-map coordinates~\cite{coifman2006} from seven daily S\&P\,500 features, fits a Koopman operator on them and tracks the spectral entropy of the embedding. Its kernel bandwidth is drawn from an unseeded random sample, so its unmodified code was rerun under 11 fixed seeds. On the original 2004--2010 data, 10 of 11 seeds reproduce the published result: the minimum spectral entropy falls on 2008-08-27, 19 days before the Lehman Brothers bankruptcy (values 1.960--1.982; published 1.972). Extended to 1992--2026, the lowest entropy in 34 years still falls in July--August 2008 for all 11 seeds (Fig.~\ref{fig:market}).

\textbf{What the Koopman view contributes.} Table~\ref{tab:summary} collects the results. For burst prediction, the Koopman framing earns its place through the log scale, the delay coordinates, the cheap online update and the spectrum, not through nonlinear observables: on scheduler data, Hankel DMD and a log-scale autoregressive model are the same predictor, and richer models add nothing. This is a useful negative result: a practitioner choosing a predictor for a scheduler should pick a log-scale linear model with enough delays and spend the remaining effort elsewhere.

\textbf{Where the gain comes from.} The practical value is in the loop. Predicted bursts, turned into EEVDF slice requests with a margin of about 1.5, made short requests 57--59\% faster on contended bare-metal cores with 1, 8 and 16 CPUs (40--45\% on the VM), cost nothing measurable in throughput when observed through eBPF, and sped up real servers' short requests by 34--77\% on bare metal (about a third on the VM). Used inside a complete sched\_ext scheduler, the same predictor turned a policy that was far worse than stock Linux into one that was 41--44\% faster. How the forecast becomes a slice matters more than which forecaster is used: the wrong margin erases the gain even for a perfect predictor, and the right one lets an imperfect predictor recover most of it. Within that, a more accurate predictor helps where slices are tight, as the model of Section~\ref{sec:margin} predicts; on bare metal, with a default slice twice as long, its advantage held up to $c=2$. On the polling agent the conformal margin helped the textbook estimator but hurt Koopman, whose wider log-scale intervals pushed slices towards the default; a fixed multiplicative margin worked better for both.

\textbf{Lessons for practitioners.} (1) Validate traces against \texttt{schedstat} before trusting them; both hazards found in this study were silent. (2) Predict bursts on a log scale. (3) If a latency-sensitive task's bursts are short, a slice request is a supported, low-risk way to serve it sooner on stock Linux, but the slice must exceed the burst with a margin; a slice equal to the burst can be worse than none. (4) A hand-set minimum slice can beat prediction for a task whose bursts are always short, but hurts tasks whose bursts vary. (5) Watch the medium-sized requests: a confident predictor gives them slices that are too short.

\textbf{Limits.} Heavy requests of real servers did not improve. On bare metal, log-EDMD made medium-sized Redis and SQLite requests 22--32\% slower and Redis's 99th percentile 21\% slower, and for some programs a hand-set slice was better. For the synthetic workload, the 99th percentile of short requests improved by about a third, while long requests took up to 3\% longer with log-EDMD at $c=1.5$. The gains need contention: on the idle lab machine nothing queued. The margin that worked best here (1.5) depends on the overhead and on the tick and preemption model of the kernel.

\textbf{Threats to validity.} The closed loop with the synthetic workload was confirmed on one bare-metal machine with a hybrid processor, using only its performance cores, and the real-server loop on a second, older bare-metal machine; other processors and kernels may need another margin. The real-server loop covers three servers on one contended core. The sched\_ext scheduler is a simple global-queue design tested on one machine with six paired runs; its comparison with stock EEVDF changes the whole scheduler, not only the prediction, which is why the same scheduler without predictions is reported alongside. The closed-loop request workload is synthetic. The bare-metal frequency test supports, but does not prove, the power-management explanation of the slowdown in Trace~3, which came from another machine. The multi-core simulator is idealized; an EEVDF simulator built for this study did not reproduce the measured closed-loop effects and is not used. Network numbers come from the original study, and the market result reproduces one published event.

%======================================================================
\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
%======================================================================
A Koopman operator on log-scale delay coordinates predicts CPU bursts better than the textbook estimator, by 29\% on real programs; it is an autoregressive model in disguise, and nonlinear observables do not improve it. Used to set EEVDF slice requests with a margin, it makes short requests 57--59\% faster on the stock Linux scheduler on bare metal with 1, 8 and 16 CPUs at negligible cost, beats the textbook estimator in 48 of 54 paired runs, and makes real servers' short requests 34--77\% faster on bare metal, at a cost to some medium-sized requests. A complete sched\_ext scheduler driven by the same predictor beat stock EEVDF by 44\% on short and 41\% on long requests. A simple model explains why the margin is needed and where predictor accuracy matters.

Further directions are a margin that adapts to the spread of each task's recent bursts, which should protect medium-sized requests; per-core queues and load balancing for the sched\_ext scheduler; evaluation on more processors; and evaluation on server workloads with service-level objectives.
